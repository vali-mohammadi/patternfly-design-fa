# Research: Iranian/Persian Visual & Product-Culture Conventions for PatternFly RTL Adaptation

**Scope note:** This research is scoped to conventions that would actually change *design/form guidance*, not general Iranian cultural background. It excludes pure text-direction/mirroring concerns (assumed to be covered elsewhere in this project).

## Summary of confidence

- **Solid, well-sourced:** National ID (کد ملی) 10-digit format and checksum algorithm (corroborated across three independent sources); mobile number format (11 digits, `09` prefix, operator prefix ranges); postal code as a 10-digit numeric code with a 5+5 structure (routing code + distribution code), corroborated across multiple Persian-language sources; the Toman-vs-Rial display convention as a real, documented product decision (Digikala); Sonnat's `direction: 'rtl'` / `rtlFontFamily` theming API as a concrete, code-level design-system artifact; the "single combined name field" pattern versus split first/last name, seen in at least one civil-registry-adjacent context.
- **Thin / uncertain — flagged explicitly below:** The precise field-by-field structure of Divar's, Snapp's, or Digikala's *own* address/checkout forms (I could not find a reliable primary or well-documented secondary source showing their actual field labels; what I found instead is Iran's national GNAF addressing system, which Digikala's help content references, plus general knowledge of Iranian address vocabulary). The internal semantic breakdown of postal-code digit positions (routing/zone/district digit-by-digit) varies slightly between secondary sources and has no confirmed primary source (post.ir) I could access. Color-symbolism findings are grounded in general Iranian-Islamic cultural/academic sources, not in any documented Iranian product-design decision — I found **no evidence of Iranian product design systems diverging from Western green=success/red=danger conventions in status-color UI**, and I say so explicitly rather than manufacturing a difference.

## 1. Name and address forms

### Name fields
- Iranian official identity documents (the *shenasnameh*/birth certificate) record فامیل (family name), نام (given name), and پدر (father's name) as distinct fields, and Iranian registration forms commonly ask the applicant to enter "نام، نام خانوادگی و نام پدر خود را مطابق با شناسنامه" (given name, family name, and father's name, exactly as on the birth certificate) — i.e., a **father's-name field is a real, recurring convention in official/serious Iranian forms** (identity verification, banking, some government registration), not something we should assume away. Source: search-aggregated description of Iranian civil-registration form conventions, e.g. discussion of شناسنامه fields and serial number use in registration forms — https://farsiha.ir/سری-و-سریال-شناسنامه/ and related results. *(Secondary, non-primary sourcing — I could not reach a live sabteahval.ir or my.gov.ir form to confirm exact field labels/screenshots; treat the "father's name field" finding as plausible-and-recurring but not verified against a live government form.)*
- In consumer-product registration contexts (not government), the pattern I found was a **single combined "نام و نام خانوادگی" (name-and-family-name) field** rather than split first/last inputs — noted in a description of Digikala's seller-registration flow ("در بخش اول فرم ثبت‌نام دیجی‌کالا، مالکان کسب‌وکار باید اطلاعات مثل نام و نام خانوادگی و ایمیل را وارد کنند" — in the first section of Digikala's registration form, business owners must enter "name and family name" and email). Source: https://selleracademy.digikala.com/مراحل-ثبت-نام-فروشندگان-در-دیجی‌کالا/ (via search synthesis). **This is a single search-engine synthesis of a page I did not fetch directly — flagged as moderate confidence, not a screenshot-verified field.**
- I was **not able to verify** the actual field-by-field structure of Divar's or Snapp's own signup/checkout forms from any accessible source (blog post, screenshot, or app-store review discussion). Searches for Divar's or Snapp's specific form layouts returned only generic marketing/how-to content about *using* the apps, not documentation of form fields. **This should be treated as an open gap** — a design-system team should pull real screenshots of these apps directly rather than relying on this research.

### Address fields
- Iran operates a national unified addressing system sometimes referred to (in the material I found) as **GNAF**, which structures an address hierarchically as: province (استان) → county/city (شهرستان) → section (بخش) → city (شهر) → and then a free-text "last passage and plaque" field (معبر آخر و پلاک) for the final street/alley/building-number detail, plus separate per-unit entries for multi-unit buildings (i.e., the system lets a user "define different units and enter information for each one" when a building has multiple units/floors). Source: Digikala Magazine article on finding a postal code from the map, describing the government address-lookup system: https://www.digikala.com/mag/find-postal-code-from-map/ (WebFetch summary of this page; I did not independently confirm "GNAF" is the correct official name/acronym, so treat that specific label as uncertain even though the hierarchical field structure it describes is plausible and consistent with how Iranian postal/address lookup tools are commonly described).
- Standard Iranian address vocabulary — confirmed consistently across multiple sources — includes کوچه (alley), پلاک (building/plot number), واحد (unit/apartment), and طبقه (floor), which do **not** map onto the "street name + city + ZIP" structure common in Western design-system address templates. Source (definitions cross-checked in the Digikala search synthesis): search summary citing https://www.digikala.com/mag/find-postal-code-from-map/.
- A UPU (Universal Postal Union) international addressing reference for Iran exists (https://www.upu.int/UPU/media/upu/PostalEntitiesFiles/addressingUnit/irnEn.pdf) but I was unable to extract readable text from it (it returned as a binary/image PDF to the fetch tool). A separate commercial addressing-format reference (Smarty) describes an international/UPU-style Iranian address as an ordered sequence: addressee name → building name → neighborhood/city-area → street → building number → city → 10-digit(ish) postal code → country, with an example "Tehran Vanak St 19958 Tehran IRN" and a commercial example "Unit 2 Tehran Tehran Kolahdoz St 2 Tehran S4P3Y2." Source: https://www.smarty.com/global-address-formatting/iran-address-format-examples. **Caveat: this is a third-party international-mailing-format reference, optimized for cross-border mail formatting, not necessarily representative of how a native Iranian user would fill out a domestic form** — its postal-code example ("S4P3Y2") looks like alphanumeric Canadian-style formatting, which does not match the confirmed 10-digit-numeric Iranian postal code (see section 3). This inconsistency suggests the Smarty page's Iran-specific data may be templated/generic rather than carefully verified, so I would treat it as low-confidence and prefer the GNAF/Digikala-derived field hierarchy and the UPU field order (name/building/area/street/number/city/postcode/country) as the more plausible skeleton, while still flagging that no fully confirmed, screenshot-verified real form was found.

**Overall for this section:** The alley/plaque/unit/floor vocabulary and the general hierarchical (province→city→section→street/alley→plaque→unit) shape of Iranian addresses is reasonably well corroborated. The exact field labels, order, and required/optional status in a specific product's live form (Divar, Snapp, Digikala) were **not** independently verified through screenshots or documented field lists — this is the weakest-sourced part of the whole research and should not be treated as ready-to-implement guidance without direct verification against live apps.

## 2. Color connotations relevant to product UI

- **Green**: In Iranian-Islamic culture, green is strongly associated with paradise, divinity, serenity, and (in the post-1979 political context) with Shia Islam and, more recently, with the 2009 "Green Movement" protest movement — i.e., green carries strong *political* connotation in some contexts, not just a positive one. Sources: Encyclopaedia Iranica, "COLOR" — https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/color-pers-rang/; academic paper "The symbolism of green color in Iranian-Islamic culture and art" — https://www.academia.edu/71975205/The_symbolism_of_green_color_in_Iranian_Islamic_culture_and_art.
- **Red**: Traditionally associated with the blood of martyrs and sacrifice in Iranian political/religious symbolism (e.g., in mourning and commemorative contexts around Ashura), in addition to more universal associations. Source: academic paper "Symbolism of the Red Color in Iranian-Islamic Culture and Art" — https://www.academia.edu/129028656/Symbolism_of_the_Red_Color_in_Iranian_Islamic_Culture_and_Art; general cultural summary — https://www.tehrantimes.com/news/396974/Shades-of-doubt-and-shapes-of-hope-Colors-in-Iranian-culture.
- **Yellow**: Historically associated with paleness/sickness in some folk contexts (e.g., the Chaharshanbe Suri fire-festival tradition of "give me your redness, take my yellowness," where yellow symbolizes weakness/illness to be discarded), but also, separately, with wisdom and royal power via the "lion and sun" (شیر و خورشید) national emblem. Source: general cultural summary, Tehran Times — https://www.tehrantimes.com/news/396974/Shades-of-doubt-and-shapes-of-hope-Colors-in-Iranian-culture; Encyclopaedia Iranica color article — https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/color-pers-rang/.

### Does this actually diverge from Western status-color conventions in *product UI*?

- **I found no documented case of an Iranian product or design system (including Sonnat, Divar's own design system) using a different color mapping for success/warning/error states.** Sonnat's own color documentation defines Error, Success, Warning, and Info status colors in conventional terms (error = "indicates errors... invalid text in a text field," success = "indicates the successful completion of an action," warning = "represents potentially dangerous actions or important messages") with no cultural caveat or deviation noted. Source: Sonnat design system docs, colors page — https://github.com/sonnat/sonnat.github.io/blob/main/pages/docs/colors.mdx.
- The cultural associations above (green=political/religious, red=martyrdom/mourning, yellow=illness/frailty) are real and documented at the level of general Iranian-Islamic culture and national symbolism, but I found **no evidence they have been treated as a reason to deviate from green=success/red=error/yellow=warning in any actual Iranian product UI**. This is consistent with a broader, well-known pattern: traffic-light-style status color conventions (red/yellow/green) are close to universal in software UI regardless of a culture's *other* symbolic associations for those colors, because users learn the software convention contextually.
- **Conclusion for this section, stated explicitly per the task instructions:** Do not manufacture a status-color divergence. The honest finding is "general Iranian-Islamic cultural color symbolism differs from Western in the ways described above (particularly green's political weight and red's association with martyrdom/mourning, which could matter for imagery, illustration, or marketing contexts), but no sourced evidence shows this changing green=success/red=danger/yellow=warning conventions in actual product UI." If the design system wants to be safe, the caveat worth carrying forward is narrower than "avoid these colors" — it's "be thoughtful about green/red in imagery, iconography, or celebratory/mourning-adjacent contexts (e.g. a large green checkmark hero graphic tied to a political movement, or red imagery near culturally mourning-coded contexts), separate from small-scale semantic status colors in form validation," but that specific recommendation is my own inference from the cultural sources, not something I found stated by an Iranian design team — flagged as such.

## 3. National ID, phone number, and postal code formats

### National ID (کد ملی)
- **Format:** 10 digits total.
- **Checksum algorithm** (corroborated across three independent sources — the official docs for the widely-used `persian-tools` npm/TS library, and two independent Persian-language developer explainers):
  1. Take the first 9 digits.
  2. Multiply each by a descending weight from 10 down to 2 (i.e., digit 1 × 10, digit 2 × 9, ... digit 9 × 2).
  3. Sum the products.
  4. Take that sum modulo 11.
  5. If the remainder is **less than 2**, the check digit (10th digit) must equal the remainder.
  6. If the remainder is **2 or greater**, the check digit must equal `11 − remainder`.
  - Additional validation rules noted in the `persian-tools` implementation: reject codes under 8 digits, reject all-zero codes, zero-pad to 10 digits, and reject codes where the 6 middle digits are all zero. An optional prefix check validates the first 3 digits against a table of city/province issuance codes.
  - Sources: `persian-tools` docs (generated from the library's actual implementation) — https://persian-tools.js.org/functions/verifyIranianNationalId.html; independent Persian-language explainer with a worked numeric example — https://academy.rayanita.com/national-code-validation-algorithm/ and https://camelcase.ir/الگوریتم-اعتبارسنجی-کدملی/ (found via search synthesis, algorithm description matches persian-tools exactly, including the worked example: for weighted first-9-digit products summing to 291, 291 mod 11 = 5, remainder ≥ 2, so check digit = 11 − 5 = 6). I could not fetch the raw persian-tools GitHub source file directly (404 on the paths tried), so the algorithm is sourced from the docs site (which is auto-generated from the library) plus independent corroborating explainers, not a directly-viewed line of source code.
  - **Design implication:** a کد ملی input field should accept exactly 10 numeric digits, with validation logic implementing the above checksum — this is a genuinely different validation shape from PatternFly's likely existing assumptions (e.g. SSN-style or generic ID formats) and worth a dedicated pattern/mask.

### Mobile phone numbers
- **Format:** 11 digits total when dialed domestically, always starting with `09`. Displayed/grouped conventionally as `09XX XXX XXXX`.
- Operator prefix ranges (third digit onward) include, per Wikipedia's telephone-numbers-in-Iran article: MCI (Hamrah-e Avval) 0910–0919 and 0990–0994; MTN Irancell 0900–0905, 0930, 0933, 0935–0939, 0941; RighTel 0920–0923; plus smaller/MVNO operators using 099X ranges (e.g. Shatel Mobile 0998-1XX-XXXX). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_numbers_in_Iran. Regulatory oversight of numbering plans sits with Iran's Communications Regulatory Authority (CRA). Source: search synthesis citing multiple telecom-reference sites, e.g. https://www.sent.dm/en/resources/phone-number-standards/ir.
- **Design implication:** an 11-digit fixed-length field starting with `09` is the correct shape for a phone-number input mask; the operator-prefix table is more useful for validation/analytics than for input masking itself, and is not required for the design system, but is available if needed.

### Postal code
- **Format:** 10 numeric digits, no letters (unlike some countries' alphanumeric postal codes).
- **Structure**, corroborated across several Persian-language secondary sources describing Iran Post's system: first 5 digits = "کد رهسپاری" (routing/dispatch code, characterizing the destination city/hub), second 5 digits = "کد توزیع" (distribution code, characterizing the specific location within that hub). A more granular breakdown found in one source: digit 1 = zone (حوزه), digit 2 = postal region (منطقه پستی), digits 3–5 = the specific area, digit 6 = building-row index, digits 7–10 = the specific location ID within that row. Sources (secondary, consistent with each other but not confirmed against a primary post.ir page): general description — https://regexpattern.com/iranian-postal-code/ (also documents digit-exclusion rules, see below); Persian-language explainer — search synthesis of results including https://m-star.blogfa.com/post/10 and https://tapin.ir/blog/tracking-code-postal-code-structure/.
- **Digit-exclusion rule** (also corroborated across two sources): in the first 5 digits, `0` and `2` are never used (reportedly to avoid visual confusion with `5` and `3` in handwriting), while `0` and `2` are permitted in the second 5 digits. A regex expressing this: `\b(?!(\d)\1{3})[13-9]{4}[1346-9][013-9]{5}\b` (also disallowing 4+ repeated identical digits). Source: https://regexpattern.com/iranian-postal-code/, corroborated independently by the Persian-language search synthesis above.
- **Caveat:** I was not able to reach an authoritative primary source (Iran Post / post.ir itself) to confirm this digit-by-digit breakdown; multiple independent secondary sources agree on the broad strokes (10 digits, 5+5 split, exclusion of 0/2 in the first 5), which raises confidence, but the fine-grained "digit 6 = building row" level of detail rests on a single source and should be treated as indicative rather than confirmed.
- **Design implication:** a postal-code field should accept exactly 10 numeric digits; if stricter client-side validation is wanted, the "no 0/2 in first 5 digits" rule and "no 4+ repeated digits" rule are usable, but confidence in the exact semantic breakdown of each digit position is moderate, not high.

## 4. Other Iranian-market UI conventions

- **RTL as a first-class theming primitive, not just CSS mirroring:** Sonnat's theming API includes an explicit `direction: "ltr" | "rtl"` theme property and separate `rtlFontFamily` / `ltrFontFamily` / `monospaceFontFamily` typography settings (with `rtlFontFamily` defaulting to/exemplified by `IRANSans`, a Persian-specific typeface, versus Roboto for LTR). This means Sonnat treats "which font renders in which direction" as a themeable design-system concern, not an incidental CSS detail — i.e., a design system serving Persian users needs a first-class concept of "the RTL font is a different typeface than the LTR font," not just a mirrored layout using the same font. Source: Sonnat docs, styling page — https://github.com/sonnat/sonnat.github.io/blob/main/pages/docs/styling.mdx. (I did not find equivalent explicit documentation of Persian-numeral handling or Jalali/Solar Hijri calendar support directly inside the Sonnat docs pages I could access — Sonnat's public docs site appears to have been archived, and the pages I could reach did not include a dedicated localization/i18n or date-format doc page. This is a gap, not a confirmed absence — Sonnat may cover this in pages I didn't reach.)
- **Currency: Toman vs. Rial is a real, documented product decision, not a cultural nicety.** Iran's official currency is the rial, but the toman (1 toman = 10 rial, historically) is the colloquially preferred unit, and this is not just spoken-language habit — it shows up as an explicit backend/frontend design decision in at least one major Iranian product: in Digikala's seller panel, sellers *enter* prices in rial, but the storefront *displays* prices to customers in toman. Sources: general toman/rial background — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_toman; Digikala seller-panel behavior — search synthesis citing https://www.zoomit.ir/tech-iran/408142-guide-to-working-with-the-seller-panel-digikala/. **Design implication:** a currency/price field or display component for the Iranian market needs to support a display unit that differs from the stored/canonical unit by a fixed factor of 10 — a distinct concern from simple currency-symbol localization.
- **Solar Hijri (Jalali) calendar is the default calendar for dates in Iranian products.** This is extremely well established as a baseline fact (Iran's official calendar), and the existence of numerous dedicated Jalali datepicker libraries/tools for Iranian web products (e.g. "Azar Datepicker," Persian-localized form builders that specifically add Jalali month/year fields) indicates that date-input components for the Iranian market need Jalali as the primary calendar system, not simply a Gregorian-calendar component with translated month names. Source: search synthesis, e.g. https://azaran-code.ir/projects/azar-jalali-and-georgian-datepicker and https://www.persian-calendar.com/, and the existence of Persian-specific Gravity Forms addons offering "Persian calendar support for month and year" fields — https://gravityforms.ir/. **This finding is at the level of "this convention clearly exists and is widely built for," not at the level of "I found a specific documented Sonnat/Divar guideline for it"** — I did not locate Sonnat-specific date-picker documentation to confirm how Sonnat itself handles this.
- **Bank-card/IBAN (Sheba) numbers as a distinct Iranian financial input pattern.** Iranian bank transfers commonly use a 24-digit IBAN-style number called "شبا" (Sheba, from شناسه حساب بانکی ایرانیان, "Iranian Bank Account Identifier"), built on the ISO 13616 IBAN standard, and it is commonly derived from/entered alongside a 16-digit bank card number (often entered as 4 groups of 4 digits). Source: search synthesis of multiple Iranian fintech blog posts describing card-to-Sheba conversion, e.g. https://payping.ir/blog/راهنمای-کامل-دریافت-شماره-شبا/ and https://vandar.io/blog/تبدیل-شماره-کارت-شماره-حساب-و-شماره-شبا-در-بانک‌های-مختلف. This is a *financial* input pattern rather than a general-purpose design-system pattern, but it is the kind of "real, specific convention" the task asked to surface — a design system targeting Iranian commerce/fintech contexts would need a 16-digit grouped card-number pattern and a 24-character Sheba/IBAN pattern in its input-field or masked-input guidance, distinct from the generic 16-digit Western card-number assumption only in the *additional* Sheba/IBAN format, since the 16-digit card number itself is not unusual.
- **What I explicitly did not find:** documented Sonnat guidance on RTL-specific component *behavior* beyond direction/typography (e.g. no material found on carousel/slider directionality, icon-mirroring rules, or chart/graph RTL conventions specific to Sonnat); no Iranian-designer Medium/Virgool essays surfaced describing a concrete non-mirroring convention beyond what's captured above (searches for Virgool.io essays on Iranian UI conventions mostly surfaced generic how-to and tooling content, not essays articulating documented design decisions). This is a real gap in the research, not a claim that nothing else exists.
